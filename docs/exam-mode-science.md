# Exam mode: what the research supports

Checked 2026-11-16. Exam mode's recommended plan is `recommendedExamPlan` in `Groups/GroupRules.swift` (and
`windows/rules.js`):

**25 focus · 8 break · 60 focus · 10 break · 45 focus · 20 break · 45 focus · then a walk** (2 h 55 min of focus,
3 h 33 min in all)

## The honest headline

No peer-reviewed study compares block lengths (25 vs 45 vs 60 minutes) or block orders. The research supports
the *shape* of a session, not exact minutes. So the minutes above are a judgment call inside what the evidence
allows, and anyone can type their own plan.

## What the evidence does say, and what the plan does with it

| Plan element | Evidence | How strong |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed blocks with planned breaks at all | Three experiments with university students compared timed breaks with breaks taken whenever the student wanted. None found a difference in work done. Mood results conflict: timed 24/6 and 12/3 gave less fatigue and more concentration in one (Biwer 2023); 25/5 did not help in another (Smits 2025). | Moderate that structure does no harm; mixed on whether it feels better. So every tomato can be paused or left. |
| Blocks of 25 to 60 minutes, none longer | Attention drifts down within a single 40-minute lecture (Farley 2013). Music students' solo practice was limited to about 1 to 1.5 hours without a break (Ericsson 1993). Students kept performing through 3.5 to 5.5 hour test sittings while fatigue rose (Ackerman & Kanfer 2009), so there is no hard cliff. | Inferred. Nothing picks 45 over 50 or 60. |
| Short opener (25) | None for or against. Kept because starting is the hard part. | A guess. |
| Longest block second, not last | Mental fatigue builds over hours of demanding work: after a day of hard tasks people shift toward easy, impulsive choices (Blain 2016; Wiehler 2022). The benefit of rest grows with time on task (Steinborn & Huestegge 2016). The original idea (60 minutes last) put the hardest stretch where fatigue is highest. | Inferred from fatigue studies; no study tests block order. |
| Breaks of 8 to 10 minutes after the first two blocks | Breaks of up to 10 minutes raise energy (d = 0.36) and cut fatigue (d = 0.35) but do not, on average, improve performance (d = 0.16, not significant) (Albulescu 2022, 22 samples, N = 2,335). | Strong for feeling better; not for scoring better. |
| One 20-minute break before the last block | In the same meta-analysis, longer breaks gave more performance benefit. A 20 to 30 minute break before a test raised scores, while each later hour of the day lowered them (Sievertsen 2016, 2 million tests, children aged 8 to 15, observational). | Moderate to weak (children, not adults studying). |
| No phone in breaks | A phone break left students about 20% slower and solving about 21% fewer problems afterwards than a paper or computer break (Kang & Kurtzberg 2019, 414 undergraduates). Plain rest beat every substitute activity in lab attention tasks (Helton & Russell 2015). | Moderate (one student study). |
| Move in breaks | A 6-minute exercise break in a 4-hour class raised energy (d = 0.84) (Blasche 2018). Exercise breaks in a lecture improved attention and learning (Fenesi 2018). | Moderate. |
| A walk at the end | A single bout of exercise improves long-term memory (SMD = 0.52, Roig 2013). That is exercise in general, not a walk after studying. | Moderate for energy, inferred for memory. |
| About 3 hours of focus in one sitting | The best violin students practised alone about 3.5 hours a day; the authors put the sustainable ceiling near 4 hours a day (Ericsson 1993; a 2019 replication did not reproduce that paper's main skill finding, and did not test the daily limit). | Weak. |

## What changed from the first idea (25 / 8 / 45 / 10 / 45 / 10 / 60)

Same four tomatoes and the same total focus time. Two changes: the 60 moved from last to second, and the last
break grew from 10 to 20 minutes. Both follow from fatigue building up over a session. Neither is proven by a
direct test, and the original order is a valid plan to type in.

## Popular claims that the research does not support

- **"52 minutes on, 17 off."** A company blog's look at its own users, not peer reviewed; its own later reruns
  gave 80/17 and 112/26.
- **"90-minute focus cycles."** The one direct test found no 90-minute rhythm in thinking performance (Neubauer
  & Freudenthaler 1995).
- **"Attention only lasts 10 to 15 minutes."** The paper usually cited for it (Wilson & Korn 2007) is the one
  that debunks it; Bradbury 2016 agrees.
- **"25 minutes is the optimal Pomodoro."** No study compares block lengths.
